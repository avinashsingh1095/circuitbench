Limiting current through an LED

Calculate a series resistor for one LED, then check worst-case current and resistor dissipation.

An LED is a nonlinear diode, not a fixed resistor. For a simple DC indicator circuit, a series resistor can limit current. A first estimate is R = (Vs − Vf) ÷ I, with supply voltage Vs, LED forward voltage Vf at the intended current, and current I in amperes. Use the LED datasheet's forward-voltage range at the relevant temperature and current; a nominal Vf alone does not establish a worst-case bound.

Worked example with tolerances

For a hypothetical 5.00 V supply (±5%), assumed LED Vf range 1.8–2.2 V at the target operating point, and a 330 Ω resistor (±5%), the nominal estimate at Vf = 2.0 V is (5.00 − 2.0)/330 = 9.09 mA. At the stated worst-case bounds, Imax = (5.25 − 1.8)/313.5 = 11.0 mA; Imin = (4.75 − 2.2)/346.5 = 7.36 mA. These example limits are assumptions, not specifications for a particular LED.

At the high-current corner, resistor dissipation is approximately (5.25 − 1.8)²/313.5 = 0.038 W. This is only a screening calculation: check the selected resistor's derating curve, ambient temperature, maximum working voltage, and pulse conditions. Confirm the LED's permitted current and the driver's output limits under the actual conditions.

Build and troubleshoot

  1. Read the LED datasheet and identify its anode and cathode from the drawing; do not infer polarity from appearance alone.
  2. Choose resistance using maximum supply, minimum forward voltage, and resistor tolerance; also check minimum expected current.
  3. Wire one LED and resistor in series with a current-limited low-voltage supply. Set a conservative current limit before energizing.
  4. Measure voltage across the resistor and estimate current as its measured voltage drop divided by its resistance.
  5. If the LED is dark, switch off and check polarity, continuity, supply, and breadboard rows. If current is unexpectedly high, disconnect immediately and recheck resistor value, supply, and wiring.

For parallel LEDs, do not assume one shared resistor divides current evenly: small forward-voltage differences can cause unequal branch current. Analyze each branch and its thermal conditions.

Try the LED resistor calculator. It uses an ideal single-LED model and rounds upward to an E12 nominal value; it does not include tolerances or certify a design.

Safety: This example is for low-voltage indicator circuits, not mains or high-energy designs. Do not connect an LED directly to a supply or GPIO without a current-limiting method.
