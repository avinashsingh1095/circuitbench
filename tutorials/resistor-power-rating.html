Choosing resistor power ratings

Estimate dissipation first, then interpret the component's rating under actual operating conditions.

A resistor converts electrical energy into heat. For a DC resistive element, useful equivalent calculations are P = V × I, P = I²R, and P = V² ÷ R. Use consistent units. For instance, 10 V across 1 kΩ gives 0.010 W using V²/R.

Rating is not a target

The printed or catalog power value is usually subject to specified ambient temperature, mounting, and thermal conditions. A component may need derating as temperature rises; the package and PCB copper can affect heat transfer. Check the manufacturer's datasheet curves and maximum working voltage as well as power rating.

Estimate with the real operating envelope

  1. Calculate the worst credible voltage or current, including supply tolerance and fault or startup conditions relevant to the circuit.
  2. Calculate dissipation in the resistor at those conditions.
  3. Compare with the manufacturer's rating and derating curve at the expected ambient temperature and mounting arrangement.
  4. Account for resistor tolerance and other safety or reliability requirements for the application.
  5. Measure temperature and function in a controlled prototype when suitable; measurement does not replace rating limits.

For a 9 V source across 450 Ω, current is 0.020 A and ideal dissipation is 9 × 0.020 = 0.18 W. This arithmetic alone does not establish which physical resistor package is appropriate.

Use the electrical power calculator for an ideal DC estimate. It is not a substitute for the datasheet's operating limits.

Safety: Hot components can burn or damage nearby materials. Avoid touching powered parts and do not test unattended.
