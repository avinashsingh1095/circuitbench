Choosing resistor power ratings

Calculate worst-case dissipation, then compare it with the exact part's thermal and voltage limits.

For a resistor under DC conditions, dissipation is P = V × I = I²R = V²/R. Pick the form that uses known values and keep units consistent. These relations describe resistor heating; they do not select a safe part by themselves.

Worked worst-case example

A 450 Ω resistor with ±5% tolerance is connected across a regulated 9.00 V supply. Nominal current is 9/450 = 20.0 mA and nominal power is 9²/450 = 0.180 W. At minimum resistance, 427.5 Ω, power is greatest: 9²/427.5 = 0.1895 W. A nominal 0.25 W resistor's simple arithmetic ratio is 0.1895/0.25 = 75.8% of its rating. That ratio does not establish suitability: the manufacturer may require derating for ambient temperature, PCB mounting, pulse loading, or working voltage.

Selection checklist

  1. List the steady, startup, and credible fault voltages/current that the resistor may see.
  2. Use tolerance extremes to calculate maximum dissipation and maximum working voltage. For fixed voltage, lower resistance increases P = V²/R; for fixed current, higher resistance increases P = I²R.
  3. Read the exact series datasheet for rated power conditions, ambient-temperature derating, maximum working voltage, pulse limits, and mounting assumptions.
  4. Check nearby materials and enclosure temperature; resistor body temperature can be much higher than room temperature.
  5. Prototype at controlled low voltage where appropriate, measure after thermal settling, and do not touch energized or hot parts.

Use the electrical power calculator for the ideal DC calculation, then do tolerance and datasheet checks separately.

Important: Do not apply a universal “twice the calculated wattage” rule as a substitute for a manufacturer's derating and pulse specifications.
