Ohm's law at the bench

Relate voltage, current, resistance, and power in a simple DC resistive circuit.

For an ohmic component at a specified operating condition, Ohm's law is V = I × R. Rearranging gives I = V ÷ R and R = V ÷ I. Power in an ideal resistor is P = V × I = I²R = V²/R. Use volts, amperes, ohms, and watts consistently: 20 mA = 0.020 A.

Worked calculation

With 9 V across a 450 Ω resistor, I = 9 ÷ 450 = 0.020 A = 20 mA. Resistor dissipation is P = 9 × 0.020 = 0.18 W. If the resistor is ±5%, the current at exactly 9 V ranges approximately from 9/472.5 = 19.05 mA to 9/427.5 = 21.05 mA. The source and component ratings still need independent checks.

Where the model applies

These equations describe a resistor or other approximately ohmic element under the stated conditions. A diode or LED has a nonlinear current-voltage curve; do not model it as a fixed resistor. For AC circuits with reactance or phase shift, simple DC power equations may not give real power.

Measure and troubleshoot

  1. With power off, inspect polarity, wiring, and resistor markings. Resistance-mode measurements on a live circuit are unsafe and can be misleading in-circuit.
  2. For voltage, set the meter to an appropriate voltage mode and connect it in parallel across the component.
  3. For current, power down, move the meter lead to the correct fused current jack, then insert the meter in series. Never put a current-mode meter directly across a source.
  4. If measured current differs from the estimate, check supply voltage, resistor tolerance, wiring, meter range, and whether the load is actually ohmic.
Scope: This is low-voltage educational guidance only, not mains or high-energy equipment instructions. Use appropriately rated instruments and follow their manuals.
